# Feedback and evidence of understanding

Judge the answer against the selected goal and the readable passage. State which
reasoning is correct and which link needs work. Feedback can be short and natural;
the categories below are decisions, not mandatory response headings.

| Reply | Useful response | Evidence status |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Correct reasoning meets the selected goal | Identify the correct mechanism or inference and its scope. Complete this point or offer a deeper goal. | Mastered for this point only. |
| Correct direction, missing a necessary link | Name the correct part and the specific omitted condition or step. Give a focused hint or explanation, then ask about that link. | Partial; identify the missing link. |
| Misconception | Quote or paraphrase the mistaken claim accurately; contrast it with the source or a small counterexample, explain the correction, then check that distinction. | Unresolved until the correction appears in an answer. |
| Missing prerequisite | Explain why the reply suggests this prerequisite is blocking the target. Teach it as supplemental background and use a small check before returning to the original question. | Prerequisite and original target are tracked separately. |
| Ambiguous or internally mixed answer | Acknowledge the supported part and ask one disambiguating question about the central conflict. Do not infer a broad knowledge deficit from imprecise wording alone. | Only the clearly supported part counts. |
| “I don't know” | Explain the point, reducing the scope or changing representation, then give one achievable check. | Explained, unverified. |
| “I understand” or repetition of provided wording | Acknowledge and, if the researcher wants to continue, invite one reasoning check suited to the goal. | Explained, unverified until substantive evidence. |
| Direct explanation, skip, pause, or end | Apply the control immediately; no compulsory check before proceeding or exiting. | Retain only evidence already supplied. |

## Calibrate the check

Choose the smallest check that can distinguish understanding from the actual
misconception. A concept question can ask which of two claims matches a definition
and why. A mechanism question can ask what changes when one condition changes.
An empirical question can ask what a reported observation warrants concluding.
An argument question can ask how a premise supports the conclusion. A transfer
or teach-back is useful when that is the goal, not a prerequisite for every lesson.

For example, “the denominator makes the numbers smaller” identifies an operation
but may omit why that operation matters. Feedback should recognize the operation
and ask about the affected downstream step. Saying “division always produces a
uniform distribution” asserts a different claim and needs a targeted correction.
These are teaching examples, not facts about an unspecified paper: use the actual
source's formula and conditions before applying them.

A long answer may contain both correct and incorrect claims. Pick the one
misconception most relevant to the target, give concrete feedback, and keep the
next question focused. When a new answer resolves it, explicitly reconnect the
repair to the original question; avoid drifting into a prerequisite curriculum.

## Concise completion or exit

Mention the current goal, what was explained, what the researcher's answer
demonstrated, and any unresolved link. On a pause or end request, this is a recap,
not a new exercise. “You explained how X affects Y under condition Z” is evidence;
“you have mastered the paper” is not justified by that single answer.
